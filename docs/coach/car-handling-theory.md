# Car-handling theory for coaching

General technique knowledge the coach draws on: any car, any track. This is a synthesis in our own words from
Suellio Almeida's *The Motor Racing Book Vol. 1 – Car Handling* (the user's own copy; chapter references are
given so a human can look the detail up), his recorded coaching sessions, and Nils Naujoks' (Popometer) ACC
coaching and telemetry videos. Measurement mapping: [rotation-and-corner-structure.md](rotation-and-corner-structure.md).
How to *teach* it: [coaching-craft.md](coaching-craft.md).

---

## 1. Vocabulary that makes the rest easy

- **Rotation** = yaw rate (°/s). Understeer = *under-rotation* (the car rotates less than the steering asks),
  oversteer = *over-rotation* (more). Oversteer is always a reaction: the fronts start a rotation the rears
  can't hold. (Book III-5)
- **Neutral steer** = both axles at their optimal slip at once; the goal state. A loose car can be held there
  with inputs; an understeery car can't be brought there without over-slowing. Fast drivers prefer cars that
  can oversteer because they keep more options. (III-5)
- **Platform slip angle**: think of the chassis as one sliding body, not front vs rear. (III-27)
- **Refresh rate**: how many states per corner a driver perceives — beginners one, intermediates two (e.g.
  understeer on entry + oversteer on exit), experts four or more. Training it = deliberately inducing
  under/oversteer at each stage. (III-30)
- **Four pillars**: feeling understeer, feeling oversteer, inducing understeer, inducing oversteer. (III-30)

## 2. Corner structure

- **Stages**: early entry, late entry, early exit, late exit (braking zone before). (II-2) Early entry: begin
  the transition from peak brake; the classic mistake is too much / too fast steering. Late entry: build to
  peak rotation; hesitating here causes most exit spins. Early exit: from the MRP, accelerate; the classic
  mistake is unwinding too early. Late exit: least control, a *consequence* of everything before.
- **Entry is cause, exit is consequence.** Control is greatest before braking and shrinks with each phase; the
  exit line is what the entry allows ("line of consequence"). (II-1, II-14)
- **Maximum Rotation Point (MRP)** = peak yaw rate = minimum speed = throttle point ≈ end of braking. Not the
  apex. Rotation builds from turn-in to the MRP as one motion and then decreases while accelerating.
  (II-9, II-12)
- **Never accelerate after the apex**; the MRP is before or at the apex. Late apex ⇒ early throttle.
  Rule-of-thumb placement as a share of the corner: fast corners MRP ≈ 49 % / apex ≈ 51 %, medium 47/53, slow
  45/55. Context (a long straight after, or a corner straight after) moves it. (II-12, II-13)
- **Rotate at least half the corner on the brakes** before accelerating (≈ 55 % on entry, 45 % on exit as a
  baseline); turning in and accelerating straight away = understeer. (II-12, III-32)
- **Maximum Rotation Line (MRL)**: holding a fixed yaw rate at half-throttle "waiting until it feels ok" —
  neither slowing nor accelerating. If the car is still decelerating it must still be gaining rotation. (II-9)
- **V vs U**: low downforce → V-shape (later, progressive/"exponential" steering build, more trail);
  high downforce → U-shape (earlier/faster initial rotation, more linear steering). Double apexes follow the
  same rule: low downforce = earlier turn-in, elliptical line, more trail, apexes further apart; high downforce
  = later turn-in, rounder line, apexes closer (enough downforce can make it one apex). (II-9, II-11, III-13)
- **Bouncing off the apex**: turning in too early or too slow → reaching the inside early → stuck there with
  rotation killed. Diagnostic drill: deliberately turn more/earlier to *find* how much rotation is available;
  if the car can hit the inside early, you were slow. (II-10)
- **Compound corners**: never drive straight between linked corners (except under heavy braking); always be
  turning on the limit. Decide the sacrifice: long straight after → sacrifice the first corner; long corner
  before a short one → sacrifice the long one's exit; don't over-sacrifice tight corners. Adjust the second
  corner by moving the *first* corner's MRP (wait before accelerating, or turn in later). Most common mistake:
  forcing the car to open up more than it can between corners. (II-14) When accelerating out of the first
  part, the MRP of the next is at the change of direction; don't add steering toward a second apex while
  accelerating. (II-13, Interlagos example)
- **Elevation and camber**: compressions and on-camber add grip — take rotation (and braking) there; crests and
  off-camber remove it — be patient, brake/turn less, have the car pointed before the crest. "Compression to
  snap decompression": a dip whose end acts like a crest. If you can't see far ahead, you're on a crest; if
  you can, likely a compression. Ignoring elevation causes most crest spins. (II-15, I-5)

## 3. Braking

- **Find the limit by braking harder at the same safe point first**, then move the point later — not by
  braking later and "dealing with it". Fear blocks learning. (I-2)
- **Pressure shape**: reach peak fast (≈ one blink in high-downforce/light/stiff cars, ≈ two in low-downforce/
  heavy/soft) and consistently — a slow, variable ramp makes every braking reference meaningless. Low
  downforce: near-flat pressure to the stop; high downforce: much higher at speed, released as speed (and
  downforce) drops. Typical errors: releasing too early (under the limit mid-zone) or not releasing enough late
  (lock). (I-3, I-6)
- **Press-less technique**: release by relaxing the pushing muscles, not by lifting the foot — gives fine
  control of release rate. (I-4)
- **Light hands under braking**: hard braking, relaxed hands; let the wheel self-centre so the car stays
  laterally balanced (49/51 already costs braking). "Brake hard, light hands." (I-7)
- **Engine braking acts on the driven axle only** (rear in RWD) → downshift timing changes the effective bias
  ("dynamic final braking bias"). Earlier/harder downshifts = more rotation; later = calmer. Prefer the last
  downshift *before* turn-in — downshifting during turn-in can start a micro-slide that snowballs. In FWD the
  effect is opposite. (I-8, III-9, III-10)
- **Entry speed consistency** comes from the braking reference *and* pressure *and* gear/RPM choice; RPM sound
  at turn-in is a usable consistency cue. (II-3)

## 4. Trail braking and rotation on entry

- **Trail braking** = blending deceleration and rotation without a gap in grip usage. (III-6)
- **String theory**: steering and brake release linked as if by a string — each % of brake released becomes
  steering. Turn in slowly → release slowly; quickly → quickly. Never increase steering at constant pressure.
  Practise different ratios. High-downforce cars: two stages (release with lost downforce in the straight,
  then the string). (III-7)
- **Advanced string theory**: release according to the car's *rotation* (output), not the steering (input).
  Understeering → keep the brake a touch longer / slow the steering rate; oversteering → release faster. (III-7)
- **Terminal trail-brake pressure (T%)**: the pressure left when the throttle starts — anything from ~3 % to
  ~20 % depending on car; lower on low grip/off-camber, higher in compressions. (III-8)
- **Three tools for rotation**: steering, trail braking, engine braking (plus rarely a "maintenance" 5–15 %
  throttle in cars that over-rotate even when coasting). Entry: steering gentle, brake + engine braking do
  most of it; deep in the corner engine braking and brake pressure fade, steering takes over. Drivers who only
  use steering get stuck. (III-9)
- **Weight transfer levels**: braking while turning — light (more rotation), ideal (slight oversteer; balance by
  releasing → induces understeer), excessive (locking; result depends on bias/ABS). Throttle on exit — under
  traction limit (understeer), at the limit (controllable oversteer, the optimum), over (spin). (III-11)
- **"Make it rotate" vs "let it rotate"** depends on brake bias / car: with forward bias + ABS, releasing the
  brake *adds* rotation (fronts breathe); in other cars it reduces it. Test this early on every new car. (III-17, III-18)
- **Brake release for balance changes speed too** (double reaction): more trail for rotation also slows you;
  releasing to cure oversteer carries more speed. Don't fluctuate pressure; the cleanest trail has the fewest
  corrections. (III-20)
- **Fishing for grip** (very oversteery cars): turn in with less force, manage the rear with the steering, add
  lock as speed drops. (III-21)
- **Inducing oversteer on entry** (learn to spin on purpose): less steering, more brake/engine braking. Inducing
  understeer: release brakes quicker, turn very quickly (scrub), or a touch of throttle. Learning to *cause* a
  behaviour is how you learn to prevent it. (III-23, III-29)

## 5. Steering

- **Steering force vs angle**: think in force (what the wheel resists) rather than degrees; force tells you
  front grip. **Light hands technique**: just enough force to steer; add less force on entry, more mid-corner
  to test grip. The harder you brake, the lighter the hands. (III-12, III-14)
- **Passive vs active counter-steer**: with light hands the wheel self-corrects small slides instantly
  (passive); active counter-steer is a ~200 ms human reaction that takes the fronts off the job. Aim for passive.
  (III-14)
- **Exponential vs linear steering**: low downforce / strong engine braking → small initial input, rising rate;
  high downforce → more linear, faster initial rate. Crests: gentler; compressions/banking: faster. Don't
  prescribe a trace — teach the feel of neutral steer and let the trace follow. (III-13)
- **Too much steering**: past optimal front slip more lock = less grip, more heat ("cooked fronts" snowball).
  If the car isn't turning with more steering, stop adding it. (III-32; Almeida sessions)

## 6. Corrections — order of priority

**Entry oversteer:** (1) release the brake a little quicker → understeer induced, fronts stay working;
(2) passive counter-steer (light hands); (3) only then active counter-steer (after ~0.3–0.4 s). (III-15, III-17)
**Exit oversteer:** (1) small throttle lift (10–20 %); (2) passive counter-steer; (3) active. Correct with pedals
before steering. (III-16, III-19)
**Snap risk zones**: early entry (load + tense hands) and late exit (accelerating while asking for constant
rotation). (Almeida video; III-31)
**Smooth is fast?** Inputs are smooth when it's ideal, but corrections must be fast and then return to plan;
slow, "gentle" corrections let slides grow. (III-28)

## 7. Throttle

- **Two parts**: initial application to the traction limit (a quick "kick" can buy a little rotation) and then
  modulation along the limit. Rate depends on horsepower (low power: almost vertical; high: progressive). (III-24)
- **Never unwind before accelerating; unwind a fraction of a second after** throttle goes in. Rotation
  decreases as speed rises; take the rotation at low speed. Fear of exit oversteer → unwinding early → too
  little rotation → forced rotation at higher speed → spin. Drill: reach a lower initial throttle on purpose
  (feel understeer), then raise it until slight oversteer. (III-25)
- **Throttle understeer** usually = squeezing too gently (weight goes back without enough rear stress) or not
  enough rotation done on entry. **Throttle oversteer** = past the sweet spot, or entry rotation lacking. (III-32)
- **FWD**: power causes understeer; rotate on entry, minimum steering on exit. (III-26)
- In ACC specifically, 40–50 % throttle may only cancel engine braking — "enough throttle to actually
  accelerate" (Popometer).

## 8. Overdriving, hesitation, setup

- **Speed first, rotation after** (brake later and deal with it) vs **rotation first, speed after** (find the
  rotation limit at slightly lower speed, then raise entry speed). The second is safer and faster for learning
  a new car/track. Judge the limit as rotation × speed: if not at the rotation limit, carry more speed; if the
  car refuses to rotate, carry less. (III-1, III-3)
- **Driving the line vs driving the car**: line-drivers sit under the limit; car-drivers seek lateral grip and
  find out quickly when they're slow (they hit the inside early). In the sim, drive the car. Artificially using
  all the exit by unwinding can hide being under the limit. Adjust entry speed with braking only. (III-2)
- **Hesitation** comes in two kinds: the consistent under-limit driver (needs to practise over-rotating in low-
  speed corners, get used to small slides, shorten post-slide hesitation) and the over-driver (brakes too late,
  needs to brake slightly earlier and focus on rotation at lower speeds and exit). Both: force rotation toward
  the centre of the corner *before* forcing entry speed. (III-29)
- **Change the driving or the setup?** Try driving adjustments first (more rotation via technique); a setup that
  feels better isn't necessarily faster — confirm with the lap time/delta. (III-4)
- **Setup tendencies**: know whether a car oversteers on entry and understeers mid-corner etc., and pre-adjust
  instead of reacting. Describing the car's tendencies precisely is a mark of a good driver. (III-31)

## 9. Problem → likely cause → what to try (III-32, condensed)

| Problem | Likely causes | Try |
|---|---|---|
| Can't hit the apex, lose the car or run wide when forcing it | Too fast (braking too late); turning in too late | Brake earlier; turn in earlier and more progressively (V-line) |
| Over-slowing into the corner | Turning in too late and holding brake too long, building an arc tighter than needed | Turn in earlier, release sooner, carry more speed, use all the track |
| Losing the rear on entry | Too much rotation from all three tools | Less trail / later downshift (induce understeer); release slightly quicker; if alternating US/OS, just too fast |
| Understeer on entry | Too much or too little brake (sweet spot); steering too fast/too much | Find the trail sweet spot; slower steering; when braking turn slowly, when not braking turn faster; RWD: slightly quicker downshift |
| Understeer on power | Throttle too gentle; not enough entry rotation | Quicker initial kick to the traction limit; more rotation on entry (~55/45) |
| Oversteer on power | Past the throttle sweet spot; entry rotation lacking | Same squeeze speed, lower initial %; fix entry rotation |
| Unpredictable under braking | Gripping the wheel; turning in too late | Relax hands in proportion to braking force; turn in slightly earlier and slower |
| Overdriving (new driver) | Braking too late | Get rotation at turn-in, release quicker but keep some pressure, try throttle variants — with the *same* braking point |

## 10. Learning (Book "General Guidelines")

- ~5 % learning, ~95 % practising until it's subconscious. Work on one or two things at a time.
- **Efficiency**: best outcome for least energy; think about one or two root inputs, the rest follows.
- **Habits**: bad habits get buried under compensations (including setup); fixing them feels wrong at first.
- **Accelerators**: awareness of what you did and why; getting the right information; causing the mistake on
  purpose. **Enemies**: hesitation (fear inhibits learning), passive driving (waiting for the car).
- **Testing a new technique**: exaggerate it first so you can feel the difference, give the other inputs time to
  adapt, expect it to feel wrong and slow at first.
- **Plateaus** happen when you repeat the same thing or don't know what else to try — learn something new or have
  someone watch you. Gains get smaller as you approach the limit: 0.05 s per corner is a lot.
- Vision: planning vision (≈ 1 s ahead, not further) alternated with quick assessment glances; blind corners —
  look at what you can see and infer the rest. (I-1; Almeida coaching session)
- Comfort and posture: relaxed body, pedal ergonomics (knee slightly bent at full brake, press with the ball of
  the foot at the same spot), 9-3 hands, no ego in the car.
